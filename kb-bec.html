NOTE 16 / Security operations

Business email compromise_

Concept Scenario study

IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Business email compromise is a scam that uses a believable business conversation to trick someone into sending money, sharing information, or changing an important process.

Recognize the attack

A message appears to come from a supplier: “Our bank details changed; please use this account.” It may contain no malware and no suspicious attachment. The attacker wants the employee to perform the harmful action willingly. BEC is an attack scenario, not an email security framework.

The message might use a forged sender, a similar-looking domain, or an actual compromised mailbox. Attackers can reply inside an existing conversation and copy its tone. A familiar name, a real signature, and passing email authentication do not establish that a new payment instruction is legitimate.

Start with the requested action and exposure

  1. Ask the recipient to pause the requested action and avoid further interaction with the suspicious message.
  2. Determine what already happened: reply, click, download, credential entry, MFA approval, data disclosure, or payment.
  3. Preserve the original message and useful identifiers, headers, timestamps, and relevant conversation history.
  4. Verify the business request through a known independent contact method, such as a number already in approved supplier records. Do not use new contact details supplied by the suspect message.

Investigate more than the header

Inspect the sender domains, Reply-To, authentication results, links, and message delivery. Search for other recipients and related messages. If a mailbox may be compromised, examine sign-ins, mailbox rules, forwarding, sent messages, permission changes, and other relevant audit activity with the tools available. Distinguish what is observed from what is still suspected.

SPF, DKIM, and DMARC help evaluate domain use. They do not clear a legitimate but compromised account or a well-configured lookalike domain. The authenticity of a payment change still needs independent business verification.

Match the response to what happened

ExposureTypical response work within the incident plan
Message received, no interactionEvaluate and scope the message; remove or quarantine confirmed malicious copies as authorized and explain the finding to the user.
Credentials entered or suspicious MFA approvedCoordinate account containment, credential and session response, authentication-method review, and investigation of account activity.
Attachment executedInvestigate the endpoint and consider immediate containment using the available evidence and authority.
Payment or sensitive data already sentEscalate immediately through the organization’s incident and business response process. For payment fraud, the responsible team should promptly contact its financial institution.

Make the handoff useful

Record the claimed sender, actual domain, recipients, actions the user took, evidence, affected accounts or devices, containment already completed, and the owner of each next step. A high-impact event may need containment and escalation while further confirmation continues. Avoid a rule such as “always wait until completely certain” that could delay an urgent response.

PUT IT INTO WORDS

Check your understanding

A supplier’s email passes DMARC and asks for new bank details. Is it safe to approve the change?

Show an example answer

No. A compromised supplier mailbox or a lookalike domain can pass authentication. Pause the change, verify it through an existing trusted contact method, and investigate the message and any user interaction. Escalate promptly if money or sensitive information has already been sent.
